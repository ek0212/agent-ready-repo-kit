# Technical Document Patterns

Load only the pattern needed for the requested document. Follow an authoritative template when one exists.

## Instructions, SOPs, Runbooks, And Manuals

1. Title that names the task.
2. Purpose, audience, scope, expected outcome, and required conditions.
3. Prerequisites, materials, permissions, time, and safety notices.
4. Numbered steps in logical order, one primary action per step.
5. Verification after consequential steps and at completion.
6. Troubleshooting, rollback or recovery, escalation, and next steps.

Use active commands. Include exact quantities, paths, states, limits, and decision points. Place each warning before the hazard and beside the affected step.

Use these notice levels when the organization has no required vocabulary:

- **Note:** Important reminder or minor outcome issue.
- **Caution:** Possible equipment, data, or process damage.
- **Warning:** Possible minor injury.
- **Danger:** Possible serious or fatal injury.

## Technical Descriptions

1. Identify the object, system, or process, its purpose, audience, and context.
2. Define required terms and give a high-level overview.
3. Describe parts, functions, interfaces, specifications, and safety constraints.
4. Organize mechanisms spatially and processes chronologically unless another logic serves readers better.
5. Pair descriptions with labeled diagrams, callouts, or tables when they reduce ambiguity.

Specifications can support a description but rarely replace it.

## Technical Definitions

Build an extended definition from:

- Term and standard spelling.
- General category or genus.
- Distinguishing characteristics or differentia.
- Function, context, examples, and useful nonexamples.
- Visuals, specifications, or comparisons when they clarify meaning.

Avoid circular definitions and near-synonyms that do not distinguish the term.

## Memos, Letters, And Technical Email

Use a direct pattern for routine or action-focused messages:

1. Main point or request.
2. Necessary explanation and evidence.
3. Action, owner, deadline, and goodwill close.

Use an indirect pattern for sensitive news:

1. Relevant context.
2. Fair explanation.
3. Main message.
4. Action and respectful close.

Use a specific subject line. Keep each paragraph focused. Include a salutation, closing, and signature when the channel requires them.

## Incident Reports

Follow the organization's form and escalation policy. Capture:

- Who was involved and who witnessed the event.
- What happened before, during, and after.
- When and where it happened.
- Injuries, damage, affected systems, and immediate actions.
- Relevant conditions, records, photos, diagrams, and external reports.
- Reported explanations, labeled by source.

Write in chronological order. Separate observation from inference. Avoid blame, judgment, speculation, and unsupported root-cause claims.

## Project Proposals

1. Introduction: need, reader benefit, and requested authorization.
2. Background: problem, context, prior work, and evidence.
3. Project description: scope, method, location, owners, schedule, deliverables, and expected results.
4. Supporting argument: feasibility, risks, alternatives, and relevant qualifications.
5. Budget and resource requirements.
6. Conclusion: value, next decision, and approval request.
7. Appendices for supporting material.

Answer the request for proposal or assignment specification exactly.

## Recommendation Reports

Use front matter only when required. In the body:

1. Define the problem, decision, audience, and criteria.
2. Explain methods and evidence sources.
3. Present results without hiding unfavorable findings.
4. Compare alternatives against the same criteria.
5. Recommend an action supported by the analysis.
6. State costs, risks, limitations, implementation needs, and next steps.

Put references, detailed data, tools, and supplemental evidence in back matter when they interrupt the main decision path.

## Progress Reports

Keep the report brief and organize it around time:

- **Completed:** Work and findings since the last report.
- **Current:** Work in progress, owners, and status.
- **Next:** Planned work before the next milestone.
- **Risks:** Blockers, decisions, dependencies, and schedule or budget effects.
- **Timeline:** Completed, active, and upcoming tasks.

Use past, present, and future tense consistently for those sections.

## Performance Evaluations And Reflections

1. State the goal, responsibility, or standard.
2. Describe what happened and why.
3. Support the result with specific evidence.
4. Identify what worked, what did not, and contributing factors.
5. State the concrete change or action for future work.

Make feedback observable and actionable. Compare performance with stated goals, not unrelated people.

## Abstracts

Follow venue requirements. Cover the purpose or problem, method or approach, principal results, and conclusion or implication when applicable. Keep details proportional to the source document and do not add claims.

## Executive Summaries

Write for the decision-maker who may not read the full report:

1. Lead with the principal finding.
2. State the problem and why it matters.
3. Summarize the method and strongest evidence in report order.
4. Give the recommendation.
5. Justify the action in terms the audience values.

Keep the summary accurate, self-contained, and usually within 5 to 10 percent of the full document when no other limit is specified.

## Plain-Language Summaries

For a planned study, explain:

- The question and why it matters.
- The evidence gap.
- Proposed method, setting, population, and duration.
- Expected findings, benefits, use, and limits.

For a completed study, explain:

- The question and why it mattered.
- What researchers did.
- What they found.
- What the results mean.
- What should happen next.

Use everyday words, short sentences, active voice, informative headings, and person-centered language. Define unavoidable specialist terms on first use.
